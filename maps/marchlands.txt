# A wooded ridge splits the march in two; four passes are the only fast ways over.
# Seats sit at the corners in reading order — 1 NW, 2 NE, 3 SE, 4 SW — and this is
# the board built for the alliance grouping: `--sides=1+2v3+4` puts a pair on each
# side of the ridge, so allies share a front instead of standing back to back. The
# corner convention and that pairing agree rather than fight — 1 and 2 *are* the
# north, 3 and 4 the south — and each pair still keeps its own purse.
#
# The fair duel stays the opposite-seat pair (1&3, 2&4); here it is simply the
# longest march, corner to corner across the ridge. Fairness is not a quarter turn:
# 22x16 is not square, so no 90-degree rotation maps this board onto itself. It is
# laid out under the whole rectangle's symmetry instead — north/south flip,
# east/west flip and the half turn — which carries all four corners onto each other,
# so every pairing is fair by construction: the 2v2 across the ridge by the flip,
# the opposite-seat duel by the half turn. That is a superset of what a quarter turn
# would have bought, and it is the fairness this board is *for*.
#
# No `# symmetric` tag: that lint is a 180-degree *duel* instrument (FP6/R5), and
# four seats have no opposing pair for it to check.
#
# Land only, like the other multi-army boards: the computer cannot plan a ferry, so
# a board it may have to fight across in any grouping lets every army walk to every
# other. The mountain shoulders wall the ridge for anything on treads; the four road
# passes (x = 3, 8, 13, 18) and the woods flanking them are the whole crossing, and
# the lateral road behind each pair is how an ally reinforces the lane next door.
# Symbols: . plains  F woods  M mountain  = road  C city  B base  Q HQ
